# LinkedIn posts for the investor audience

Not published on the site. Three posts written to turn PE, M&A and investor
connections into direct messages, plus the reply kit and a DM template for
new connections. Copy each post as plain text. Post one per week, Tuesday to
Thursday, morning UK time.

Before posting anything, apply the profile copy from
`linkedin-repositioning.md` (headline, About, featured links), so anyone who
clicks through lands on the investor story.

No external links in post bodies. LinkedIn suppresses them. Links go in the
reply kit and, if wanted, a first comment.

## Post 1: The checklist offer (week 1)

There are eight things I check before money is committed to a software company.

Most of them are not in the data room.

Three examples.

Who owns the source code. Founder and contractor IP is often never formally assigned. Sometimes the company does not fully own the product you are buying.

Whether one engineer holds all the knowledge. If the only person who understands deployment and production incidents leaves after completion, their knowledge leaves with them.

Whether the AI is real. Some products sold as AI are a thin wrapper around another company's model, priced as if they were proprietary.

The full checklist covers code ownership, deployment, technical debt, security, AI claims, key-person risk, cloud costs and documentation. One page, written for investors, not engineers.

If you want it, message me and I will send it. No call and no pitch, just the checklist.

## Post 2: Plain intro (week 2, then pin it to the profile)

Most of my new connections here work in private equity, M&A, or investing. A short introduction, so you know when I am useful to you.

I run Daniel Molloy Ltd, an independent technology advisory practice. Investors ask me to look at the technology behind a deal. I tell them what I find, in plain English.

The work falls into three situations.

Before a deal. I review the code, architecture, security, cloud costs, team and AI claims, then give the deal team a report with risk ratings. A red-flag review takes five to seven days. A full report takes two to three weeks.

Inside the portfolio. When a technology company is not delivering and the board cannot tell why, I find out and say so plainly. Where it helps, I stay on as a fractional CTO with a defined end.

Before an exit. A sell-side review, so problems are found and fixed before a buyer finds them.

There is no software to sell and no delivery target behind the advice. Every engagement is confidential.

If you are looking at a software company, or have one in the portfolio you are unsure about, message me. Talking it through costs nothing.

## Post 3: What legal DD cannot tell you (week 3)

Legal due diligence is essential on a software deal. It does not answer every technical question.

Contracts can confirm who owns the IP.
They cannot tell you whether the codebase is maintainable.

Data clauses can identify legal exposure.
They cannot tell you whether the platform can support the growth plan.

An AI clause can describe a product claim.
It cannot tell you whether the AI is real, defensible, or a thin wrapper around commodity tooling.

That is why technical due diligence should sit alongside legal due diligence.

I review the source code, architecture, security, cloud costs, technical debt, team dependency and AI claims behind a deal. The output is written for deal teams, not engineers: risk ratings, commercial implications, remediation actions, questions for management and post-close priorities.

A red-flag review takes five to seven days. A full report takes two to three weeks. Every engagement runs under NDA.

If you are looking at a software company now, message me. One conversation is usually enough to tell whether a deeper review is worth it.
